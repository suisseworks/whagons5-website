1. Introduction and Purpose

Qualifying a high-temperature, high-pressure (HP/HT) subsea pipeline for service takes more than a single check. Beyond the risk of an uncontrolled lateral (global) buckle at a random location, typically the subject of its own dedicated assessment, the pipeline must also be shown adequate for local buckling at every free span and geohazard crossing along the route, for fatigue accumulated from ordinary operating cycles, for safe accommodation of the axial expansion it develops at each termination, and for freedom from cumulative axial “walking” over repeated thermal cycles. Each of these is a distinct failure mode with its own governing condition, and a pipeline that passes one check is not automatically shown adequate against the others.

Cronus has developed and repeatedly applied a structured, full-route methodology that qualifies a subsea pipeline against all of these mechanisms together, using one consistent global model and one consistent set of soil and tension assumptions across every check. This white paper describes that methodology: the engineering basis it requires, the analytical framework it follows, the criteria it evaluates, and the deliverables a client receives.

The methodology is illustrated in Section 7 using a hypothetical example pipeline. That example, including all dimensions and results, is fictitious and constructed solely to demonstrate how the framework is applied; it is not drawn from, and does not represent, any actual Cronus project or client.

4. The Cronus Thermo-Mechanical Qualification Methodology

Cronus qualifies the full pipeline route with a single global finite element model, run through the pipeline's actual load history, evaluated under a defined matrix of soil-resistance and tension cases to identify the true governing combination rather than assuming the most conservative single case controls every check.

4.1 Full-Route Load-History Simulation

A global model of the pipeline, seated on the true 3-D seabed terrain, is stepped sequentially through its actual construction and operating history: lay, flood, hydrotest, de-pressure, de-water, nitrogen pack (where applicable), operation at both minimum and maximum flow, shut-in, and, for strength purposes only, a bounding blowdown condition. Repeated turndown/ramp-up and shutdown/restart transient cycles are layered onto the operating phases to capture their contribution to fatigue and to end-expansion cycling.

4.2 Case Matrix

Cronus does not run a single bounding case. Instead it defines a small matrix of soil-resistance and lay-tension combinations, for example best-estimate resistance paired with best-estimate tension as the most likely global response, alongside combinations that isolate the worst local response, the longest free spans, and the governing lateral-buckle-fatigue condition. Each case considers the full variation of pipe wall thickness, geotechnical province, and bottom tension along the route. Running the matrix, rather than a single assumption, is what lets Cronus identify which combination actually governs at each location. That combination is not always the one that looks most conservative at first inspection.

4.3 Strength (Local Buckling) Check

At every node, phase, and case, the combined bending moment, effective axial force, and internal/external pressure are checked against the DNV-ST-F101 local buckling load-controlled criterion, using the safety class appropriate to that phase and location. Full corrosion allowance is applied for operating-phase checks; uncorroded properties are conservatively retained elsewhere in the model. Any global (lateral) buckles the model develops are cross-checked for consistency against the companion lateral buckling assessment, since that assessment governs the lateral-buckle-specific strength conclusion.

4.4 Fatigue Check

Fatigue damage from operational turndown/ramp-up and shutdown/restart cycling is calculated directly from the global model's stress ranges using an S-N approach per DNV-RP-C203, and then combined, by simple summation consistent with a linear damage rule, with fatigue damage from every other identified source relevant to the route: installation, vortex-induced vibration, lateral-buckle fatigue, and chemical-injection or pigging-related flow transients, each typically sourced from its own dedicated companion assessment. The result is a single, auditable, cumulative fatigue total for every governing location along the route.

4.5 End Expansion and Interface Loads

Single-cycle pipeline end expansion is calculated at both terminations across the governing cases and operating conditions. Where the predicted expansion at a sliding termination structure exceeds its physical travel limit, the resulting horizontal interface load transferred into that structure is calculated and checked against its rated capacity.

4.6 Weight and Buoyancy-Loss Sensitivity

Parametric re-runs vary pipe and content weight within manufacturing and fluid-density tolerances, and buoyancy module uplift within its as-built tolerance and a progressive accidental-loss scenario, to confirm the strength conclusion is not unduly sensitive to those uncertainties and to define an allowable buoyancy-loss threshold at each buoyed location.

4.7 Walking Assessment

Where a route's tension profile or geometry creates the possibility of cumulative axial creep (“walking”) under repeated shutdown/restart cycling, Cronus tracks the net displacement at both terminations and at any high-tension intermediate feature across several simulated cycles. A consistent, non-progressing effective-axial-force virtual anchor point confirms the route is not walking; a displacement that grows cycle over cycle indicates walking and is carried forward into the client's integrity management planning.

6. Finite Element and Simulation Approach

The full-route model is built in a dedicated pipeline design tool driven by a true 3-D seabed digital terrain model (e.g., Fugro SAGE Profile), using a nonlinear elastic pipe formulation and a pipe-soil interaction model with memory, so that embedment and resistance carry forward correctly from one load step to the next rather than resetting between phases. Running the same tool, on the same terrain model and soil basis, as Cronus’s companion lateral buckling and free-span/VIV assessments keeps all three analyses consistent with one another and avoids the reconciliation problems that arise when a route is modeled independently for each failure mode.

For this hypothetical Pipeline Z, the route is shown adequate for local buckling strength (with one location flagged for a targeted wall-thickness review), fatigue, end expansion and interface loading, weight and buoyancy-loss uncertainty, and freedom from walking, completing a full thermo-mechanical qualification built from one consistent global model and case basis.

Where This Methodology Creates Value

  • One consistent global model and case basis across strength, fatigue, end expansion, sensitivity, and walking avoids the reconciliation gaps that appear when each check is modeled independently.
  • Running a defined case matrix, rather than a single conservative assumption, identifies the combination that actually governs at each location, often showing fewer true exceedances than a first-pass conservative screen would suggest.
  • Explicit weight and buoyancy-loss sensitivity results give the client a quantified operating margin, rather than a qualitative assurance, against manufacturing tolerance and accidental buoyancy loss.
  • A dedicated walking check closes out a failure mode that is easy to overlook on a route without an obvious anchor point, before it becomes an integrity management surprise.

9. Conclusion

Cronus has applied this thermo-mechanical qualification methodology across multiple subsea gathering, export, and flowline routes spanning a range of water depths, soil conditions, and pipe sizes. The framework is deliberately built around one consistent global model and case basis, so that a client receives a single, internally consistent qualification rather than a set of independently modeled checks that must be reconciled after the fact.
