Most hotel maintenance teams spend the day reacting: a guest reports no hot water, an AC unit drips, a lift stops between floors. Preventive maintenance is the work that keeps those calls from happening, and it is also the first work to slip when the day gets busy. It is a written schedule that says what gets checked, where, how often and by whom, and a way to see which checks actually happened. This guide gives you a preventive maintenance checklist to start from and shows how to run it in Whagons.

## Try this first

**Three things for this week**

1. List every check your team already does from memory, and write next to each one how often it should happen.
2. Pick one area, like the plant room or the pool, and put only that area on a schedule.
3. At the end of the week, look at which checks were done late or not at all, and ask why.

## 1. Build the list before the schedule

### Start from your equipment, not from a template

**What to do.** Walk the property with your maintenance lead and list the equipment and areas that need regular checks: boilers and water heaters, chillers and AC units, pumps, generator, lifts, kitchen extraction, pool, fire safety equipment, roofs and drains.

**Why it matters.** A generic checklist misses what is specific to your building. The list your team writes is the one they will follow.

**How.** Use this as a starting point and cut or add until it matches your property. Frequencies for safety systems, lifts, water and gas are often set by local regulations or by the manufacturer, so check those before you decide.

```checklist
Daily: plant room walk-through, boiler and water heater temperatures and pressure, pool chemistry, generator fuel level, leaks and alarms
Weekly: generator test run, emergency lighting check, pump and drain check, guest room sample for taps, showers and lights
Monthly: AC and fan coil filters, kitchen extraction filters, lift inspection log, fire extinguishers visible and in date
Quarterly: deep room checks floor by floor, water tank inspection, roof and gutter check
Yearly: certified inspections and services required for fire systems, lifts, gas and electrical installations
```

### One check, one place, one frequency

**What to do.** Split the list so each line is a single check, at a single place, with a single frequency.

**Why it matters.** "Check the AC" is not a task anyone can finish. "Clean fan coil filter · Room 204 · monthly" is. When each line is that specific, you can assign it, schedule it and know whether it was done.

**How.** Name each check as action, object and place:

```text
Run generator test · Generator room · weekly
```

## 2. Put the schedule where the team works

### Turn the list into a plan

**What to do.** In the Maintenance space, create a plan in [Work Plans](/en/blog/work-plans) and add one schedule for each check and frequency. Group plans by area, like "Plant room" or "Pool and wet areas".

**Why it matters.** Whagons creates the tasks when they are due, in the grid the team already uses. Nobody has to remember that today is the monthly filter day.

### Select spots instead of copying tasks

**What to do.** When a check repeats across many places, like filters in every room, select all the rooms as spots in one schedule.

**Why it matters.** Whagons creates one task per spot on every run. One schedule replaces dozens of copied tasks, and when the check changes, you change it once.

### Attach the checklist and a deadline

**What to do.** Add a form to the schedule with the readings and questions for that check, and set an SLA if the team needs a deadline.

**Why it matters.** A form keeps the readings, like a temperature or a pressure, on the task with the person and the time. Without an SLA, plan tasks start with no deadline.

## 3. Check that it happened

### Review the calendar every week

**What to do.** Open the plan's Calendar once a week with your maintenance lead.

**Why it matters.** A schedule nobody reviews becomes another sheet on the wall. The calendar shows completed days in green and late days with a red triangle, so the gaps are visible without asking around.

**How.** Start with the late days. Each one is a check that didn't happen, and someone should be able to say why.

### Turn findings into corrective tasks

**What to do.** When a check finds a problem, create a separate task for the repair, with the place as its spot and a photo.

**Why it matters.** The preventive task records that the check happened. The repair needs its own owner and its own status, or it disappears inside a closed task.

## Your preventive maintenance checklist

Go through it with your maintenance lead before you switch the first plan on.

**The list**

- [ ] Every piece of equipment and area that needs regular checks is listed
- [ ] Each check has one place and one frequency
- [ ] Regulated checks follow local rules and manufacturer guidance

**The schedule**

- [ ] Checks live in Work Plans, grouped by area
- [ ] Repeated checks use spots instead of copied tasks
- [ ] Each schedule has a form and, if needed, an SLA

**The follow-up**

- [ ] Someone reviews the calendar every week
- [ ] Every late day has an explanation
- [ ] Problems found become their own repair tasks

Start with one area. When its first month comes back green, add the next one.
