A dripping tap, a dead TV remote, a broken safe, a smell of gas, a loose tile in the lobby: maintenance gets more requests than it can do at once. When every request arrives as "urgent", the technician picks whatever is closest or whoever shouted last, and the one that really mattered waits. A simple priority system fixes that. This guide covers four levels, who decides, and how to keep the queue honest. It is about reactive requests; for recurring checks, see the [preventive maintenance schedule](/en/blog/preventive-maintenance-schedule).

## Try this first

**Three things for this week**

1. Write down the last twenty maintenance requests and sort them by hand into four piles: safety, guest in the room now, before the next arrival, and can be planned.
2. Agree with the front desk and housekeeping which words mean "urgent". Write them on one page.
3. Name one person per shift who sets or corrects the priority of every new request.

## 1. Four levels are enough

Nobody remembers the difference between priority 3 and priority 4 at 7 a.m. Four plain levels cover almost everything.

### Urgent: safety or a guest affected now

**What to do.** Use Urgent for anything that risks people or property, and for problems in an occupied room that stop the guest from using it: water leaks, no hot water, no power, a door that does not lock, a gas or burning smell.

**Why it matters.** These are the requests that turn into complaints, refunds or injuries if they wait. If Urgent is reserved for them, the technician knows to drop everything else.

### Today: before the next guest or before the day ends

**What to do.** Use Today for issues in rooms due for arrival, and for anything a guest will notice but can live with for a few hours: a dead bulb, a slow drain, a TV with no signal.

**Why it matters.** A room cannot be sold honestly with these open. They connect directly to your [room readiness checklist](/en/blog/room-readiness-checklist).

### This week and Planned

**What to do.** Use This week for small defects in vacant rooms or public areas that do not block anything. Use Planned for jobs that need parts, a contractor or a room out of order for a day.

**Why it matters.** Without these two levels, small jobs either clog the urgent queue or disappear. Giving them a home keeps them visible without stealing attention.

## 2. Who decides the priority

### The reporter suggests, one person confirms

**What to do.** Let whoever reports the issue (housekeeping, front desk, a supervisor) pick a level, and have the maintenance lead or shift supervisor confirm or correct it.

**Why it matters.** The person who sees the problem knows the context: is the guest in the room, is someone arriving at three. The person who confirms knows the workload and can stop everything from becoming Urgent.

**How.** Keep the rule on one page: what counts as Urgent, what counts as Today, and an example of each from your own property.

## 3. Set response targets you can actually meet

### Targets are yours, not an industry number

**What to do.** For each level, write the time by which someone should be on the way and the time by which it should be fixed or escalated. Base them on your staffing, not on a number from someone else's hotel.

**Why it matters.** A target nobody can meet gets ignored after a week. A realistic one shows you, every day, which requests are late and why.

**How.** Review them after a month. If Urgent requests keep running late at night, that is a staffing conversation, not a priority problem.

## 4. Keep the queue honest

### One queue, with the place and a photo

**What to do.** Every request goes into one shared list, not a mix of radio calls, notebooks and chat messages. Each request names the room or area, the problem in a short line, the priority and, when it helps, a photo.

**Why it matters.** A request that lives only in a [WhatsApp group](/en/blog/whatsapp-groups-hotel-operations) has no owner and no status. A request in a shared list can be sorted, assigned and checked.

**How.** In Whagons, each request is a task on the room or area Spot with its priority, who it is assigned to and a due time. A template can fill in the priority for common problems, so "No hot water" always starts as Urgent:

```text
Leak under bathroom sink · Room 305 · Urgent
Bulb out, bedside lamp · Room 412 · Today
```

### Re-check priorities at every handover

**What to do.** At each [shift handover](/en/blog/hotel-shift-handover), read the open Urgent and Today requests aloud and confirm who has them.

**Why it matters.** Priorities change. A This week job in a vacant room becomes Today the moment the room is assigned to an arrival.

## Your maintenance priority checklist

Use it for every new request this week.

**When a request comes in**

- [ ] It names the room or area and the problem in one line
- [ ] It has a priority: Urgent, Today, This week or Planned
- [ ] Someone confirmed the priority
- [ ] It is assigned to a person, not to "maintenance"

**During the shift**

- [ ] Urgent requests are handled before anything else
- [ ] Today requests in arrival rooms are done before check-in
- [ ] Late requests are escalated, not left to wait

**At handover**

- [ ] Open Urgent and Today requests are read out with their owners
- [ ] Priorities are updated for rooms that now have arrivals

Start with one week of sorting every request into four levels. When the team stops asking "is this urgent?", the system is working.
